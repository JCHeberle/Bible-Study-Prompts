# Exegesis Style and Format Guide (11-12-2026)

## Purpose

This guide is designed to help produce clear, warm, and faithful biblical teaching for modern English-speaking believers — in three registers: *exegesis* (what the text says), *witness* (what Scripture says elsewhere), and *exposition* (what the teacher hears, always labeled). The goal is to do careful work with the original text while presenting it in an accessible, thoughtful, and spiritually nourishing way — without unnecessary technical language or an overly heavy 1st-century framing in the final prose.

**The three registers.** Every page speaks in three registers, and the labels tell the reader which is speaking:

- **Exegesis** — "this is what these verses say": the quotation, **What it says**, the **Translation Barrier**, and the **Observations** (the teaching, under its printed label; §7). Formal test: a reader holding these verses *within the flow of the chapters already read in this book (Standing Rules 2.8)* and *the complete original-language sentence in which these verses sit (Standing Rules 2.66)* could recognize it as what they say. Intra-book continuity—spending a title, verb, or household relationship the author has already established earlier in the same book—is exegesis, not a leak. In review, an addition to the exegesis is admitted only if it passes that test.  
- **Witness** — "Scripture says the same thing over here": the `### Unbroken Witness` block (§8). Intra-book witnesses from earlier in the same book may be placed in Stage 3; cross-book witnesses are placed at Integration (Standing Rules 3.2).  
- **Exposition** (the hearing) — "this is what I hear once those verses are granted": the `### Alongside this` unit (§9). Always labeled; it never masquerades as exegesis. Anything needing a fact from *outside the book under study* — another canonical book, a contemporary image, pastoral counsel — is exposition, whatever it felt like to write.

### A Note on Intent and Use of Structure

The elements described in this guide (Translation Barriers, Unbroken Witness, Alongside this, thematic titles, and other structural features) are **tools**, not requirements or goals in themselves. They exist to serve the reader’s understanding. Use them when they genuinely help clarify the meaning, significance, or impact of the text. Do not include them simply because the guide mentions them. The guiding question should always be: *Does this help the reader better understand what the passage is saying and why it matters?* Structure should follow purpose, not the other way around.

## I. Overall Tone and Voice

Write for **thoughtful modern believers**. The prose should feel like warm, clear Bible study teaching — respectful of the text and serious about meaning, but conversational and accessible.

Avoid turning the running commentary into a technical grammatical analysis. Greek and Hebrew should appear mainly in the Translation Barriers or in brief, well-explained glosses when they significantly affect meaning. Use first-person plural language naturally (“we,” “us,” “our”) when it fits the flow of teaching.

**Teaching Bridge principle**

Whenever the English translation is familiar but the Greek carries a richer or more precise force, begin with the wording the reader already knows and trusts, then gently open it up. The reader should experience an “Ah-ha” of deeper understanding rather than a sudden drop into unfamiliar language.

Gentle, caring confrontation is appropriate when the text itself is searching or when a common gap between the text’s claim and ordinary Christian experience needs to be named. Avoid both accusation and vague moralizing.

## II. Document Structure and Hierarchy

**The "No Redundant Formatting" Rule:** In the Working Draft and the Published Exegesis, never combine Markdown heading tags with bolding tags. To ensure clean portability across Google Docs, Word, and standard text editors, a heading (`#`, `##`, `###`) already dictates weight and hierarchy on the page; adding `**` inside a heading creates visual clutter and rendering errors. A finished sample that writes `## **Apostolic Lens**` or `### **Unbroken Witness**` is not the pattern. Do not copy the bold into the heading. Thematic titles inside a section are bold lines, not headings. Those may be bold. Headings may not.

### 1\. Main Title

Use a clear, descriptive title with a single hash (`#`) and no bold tags. When working with shorter passages or specific sections, include the verse range.

- **Correct:** `# Romans 6:1–2: Dead to Sin`  
- **Incorrect:** `# **Romans 6:1-2: Dead to Sin**`

### 2\. Introduction (Apostolic Lens)

Open with an introduction under `## Apostolic Lens` that sets the context and orients the reader. The length and content should be determined by the needs of the passage. The introduction is flexible and may incorporate other structural elements when they serve understanding.

The Lens is not author identification; it is "how we got here." The Lens answers one question: what did the previous chapters set up that this chapter answers, assumes, or reveals — the letter's argument arriving here, so the reader feels why the chapter seems obvious as they read it. In a book's opening chapter the Lens may identify writer, recipients, and occasion; deep in a book it must not re-identify — the reader knows the writer by chapter 12\. What the Lens may claim: the argumentative momentum of the book to this point, the world of the hearers where it bears on that momentum, the chapter's movement in a few breaths; honest alternate views where the chapter requires them. What it may not do: teach the chapter's groups, spend Stage 3 hearings, carry exposition or teaching-note material. One exception, Standing Rules 3.3: the Lens may carry that close, under that rule's three conditions. It still may not teach the groups or spend the hearings. It names the close and gets out of the way. The Lens may use whole-chapter knowledge for orientation — naming where the chapter is going is orientation — but it must not arrive before the text does; it orients, then gets out of the way. At Integration, the Lens is written or recast fresh for the assembled chapter. Stage 3 groups do not write a Lens.

### 3\. Major Sections

Divide the passage using Roman numeral headings (`##`). These should reflect the major movements or turning points in the text established in Stage 1\. Prefer full descriptive titles that help the reader see the movement of the argument.

- **Correct:** `## I. The Law's Jurisdiction Ends with Death`  
- **Incorrect:** `## **I. The Law's Jurisdiction Ends with Death**`

### 4\. Thematic Titles Inside a Section

A thematic title is the paragraph-group title. It is a bold line, not a heading. Do not use Markdown heading tags (`###`) for it. The title stands on its own line and does not carry the verse range; each blockquote carries its own range as the bold label on the line above it (§5).

**Preferred pattern:**

`**Through the Body of the Messiah**`

`**(Romans 7:4)**`

Where a long teaching takes up different parts of the verse in turn, the title may carry the verse reference for the part in view.

The major section names its whole range once, at the open. The actual quotations are distributed through the section, at the joints (corresponding to the Stage 2 groups). Each verse block carries its own stack: quotation, **What it says**, **Translation Barrier** if earned, **Observations** if there is anything left to say — then its Witness if it has one, then its **Alongside this** if a hearing was produced for it. Do not quote the whole section in one block. That produces one Barrier farm. Each block stays tightly coupled to its verses: the reader never holds a verse in mind across intervening blocks to find what was said about it.

Block size is governed by syntactic sentence unity and Barrier load, not arbitrary verse counts. A block carries a complete syntactic thought-unit whose Translation Barrier remains a tight, readable list. When the Tier 2 and Tier 3 problem words in a marathon sentence would make the Barrier unwieldy, split the block at a natural clause joint.

Not a requirement. A cluster does not get a title because it is a cluster. Use one when the teaching of a quotation would otherwise be a wall of text, or when a short name would help the reader anticipate the subject about to be entered. It anticipates. It does not spend the teaching. A short paragraph does not need one. Do not invent a title to fill a slot. A section with none is complete.

A section may also open with a thematic lead-in: one bolded pull-sentence from the passage's own wording, after the section range and before the first blockquote. That lead-in is optional. It is not a substitute for a thematic title, and a title is not required because a lead-in was skipped.

### 5\. Quoted Text

Use the **supplied English version**: the translation the user names when the chapter is commissioned, and the **NASB 2020** when none is named. Every Framework file that says "the supplied English version" means this one. Name the section range once, as a bold parenthetical under the section heading — `**(Titus 2:1–10)**`. Place the text of each distributed cluster inside its own standard Markdown blockquote (`>`), not the whole section in one quote. Give each blockquote its verse reference as a bold label on the line above it: `**(2:1)**` for a single verse, `**(2:3–5)**` for a range.

- **Bold** the minimal English word(s) in the quoted text that also appear in the Translation Barrier below it (Standing Rules 2.72). This visual link is required. Verse numbers inside the quotation stay plain (`> 1`, `> 2`) — bold outside the quote is reference, bold inside is meaning. Do not bold verse numbers.  
- **Why verse numbers stay inside the quote block even when the range is printed above it:** The Barrier and the teaching regularly refer to what happens in a specific verse within the block (e.g., contrasting *every* in 3:1 with *every* in 3:2). Without the plain numbers `1`, `2` sitting inside the quote text itself, the human reader cannot see where verse 1 ends and verse 2 begins.  
- Use clean Markdown formatting so that the blockquotes copy cleanly into standard word processors.

**Example:**

**(2:3–5)**

> 3 Older women likewise are to be **reverent** in their behavior, not **malicious gossips** nor enslaved to much wine, teaching what is good, 4 so that they may **encourage** the young women to love their husbands, to love their children,

### 5a. What it says (plain-English restatement)

Directly after each blockquote, print the accepted Rule 2.05 restatement under the bold label `**What it says**` — a bold line, not a heading (the "No Redundant Formatting" rule above; it matches `**Translation Barrier**`). As many plain sentences as the verses require for coverage: what a first-century hearer would have understood these verses to be saying. The test is coverage — every clause of the verse span rendered in plain English, with the bold-word mappings intact. There is no sentence quota and no typical length. A restatement is finished when every clause is present and no further sentence would advance the meaning.

**Why `What it says` uses full phrases instead of a single slick synonym:** Published English Bibles look smooth only because the translators silently amputated half of the Greek word's range or picked one side of an open grammatical form to keep the line short. **What it says** is not competing with the NASB for brevity; its job is to hand the English reader the full, un-amputated sight of what a first-century hearer actually heard—including both sides when the Greek genuinely leaves both open. Never "polish" a restatement by trimming away the very sight it was built to recover.

This block is the meaning, stated before it is taught. It is not commentary and not exposition; no claim enters here that the restatement halt did not accept. The label does the differentiating work every printed book already does: verse, then what it says, then the notes when there are notes, then the observations when there are any. "Plain" here means ordinary words, not "no bold." This block is still what the verse says, not a second Barrier. A reader skimming must never confuse the restatement with the narrative that follows it.

In **What it says**, put in bold the minimal rendering word(s) you are using for each bold word in the quotation (Standing Rules 2.72). Where a bold word stands more than once in the quotation, bold its rendering at every occurrence here — one marking for each occurrence, matching the quotation's own bolding. If it takes a short phrase to say it, bold that phrase. Do not bold a second phrase for the same word, and do not bold the whole sentence. If a cluster has no Barrier, nothing in **What it says** is bold.

The restatement is one paragraph. The first paragraph break after the label is the end of it. What follows is the Barrier if one was earned, then the **Observations** if there are any — neither label is ever printed over nothing (§7).

The teaching does not start by repeating what **What it says** has already said. Its first sentence adds something.

### 6\. Translation Barrier

Place this section directly after **What it says**. Use a clean vertical bulleted list with minimal extra whitespace between items. Focus on Tier 2 and Tier 3 words — terms that carry significant theological weight or where the English translation may flatten or distort the Greek or Hebrew meaning (Standing Rules 2.65).

- Strictly avoid LaTeX formatting. On the reader's page, every Greek or Hebrew word appears in italic English transliteration, never in Greek or Hebrew letters: **Law** (*nomos*). Mark long vowels, following the SBL Handbook academic style (Greek *ē* for eta and *ō* for omega; Hebrew macrons where no vowel letter is written, as in *dābār*, and circumflexes where one is written, as in *tôrâ*), using standard Unicode letters. Greek and Hebrew letters stay only in files the AI reads, never on the reader's page: the Framework, Standing Rules, Style Guide and Few-Shot rule books, Stage 2 audits, the Knowledge Base, worker notes, and Craig's NA28, LHB, BDAG and HALOT pastes.  
- Follow the Unicode with a clear, thought-for-thought explanation. Keep the explanations concise and readable.  
- The transliterated word in parentheses belongs to the headword. The explanation after the dash is the first thing the reader is taught (Standing Rules 2.5).  
- Use the index-pair headword format from Standing Rules 2.72 (`**quote-word** / **rendering-word**`, or the single `**quote-word**` if the rendering keeps the word unchanged), matching the bolded words in the quotation and **What it says**.  
- **Why this index-pair is essential for the human reader (Never drop or "simplify" it):** The Translation Barrier is the visual key that bridges the printed English Bible to **What it says**. Often an English translation uses the exact same English word twice in one sentence for two different Greek meanings (e.g., Titus 3:1–2 **"every"** → **"each"** in 3:1, but **"every"** → **"in every instance and in full measure"** in 3:2), or swaps English words across two different Greek roots (e.g., Titus 3:2 translating *epieikeis* as **"gentle"** and *prautēta* as **"consideration,"** so when **What it says** restores *prautēta* to **"gentleness,"** the reader would think **"gentleness"** came from **"gentle"** unless the Barrier maps both sides: `**gentle**/**gracious, forbearing, fair-minded**` and `**consideration**/**gentleness**`). Without both sides of the slash-pair bolded in the Barrier headword, the human reader cannot track which bold word in the NASB maps to which bold word/phrase in **What it says**.

**Preferred form of each bullet:**

- **Law** (*nomos*) — here the concrete body of commandments that exercises real authority over those who live under it; not an abstract “principle” or “system,” but the living demand that rules the person.

### 7\. Teaching (printed as Observations)

The Teaching is the exegesis in narrative voice: what these verses say, taught warmly. On the page it is printed under the bold label `**Observations**` — a bold line, not a heading, matching **What it says** — on every verse block that carries observations prose, whether or not the block carries a Translation Barrier. The label is what shows the reader that the restatement has ended and the observations have begun; the prose is never labeled "Teaching" or "Exposition" on the page — "Exposition" belongs to `### Alongside this` (§9) alone. No label in the block is ever printed over nothing: a block with no Barrier omits the Barrier, and if a block ever has no observations — nothing left that the restatement and Barrier have not already said — it omits the **Observations** label too. It must pass the exegesis test — a reader holding these verses *within the flow of the chapters already read in this book (Standing Rules 2.8)* and *the complete original-language sentence in which these verses sit (Standing Rules 2.66)* could recognize it as what they say. Intra-book continuity with earlier chapters of the same book belongs to exegesis and requires no disclaimer label. The Teaching never imports a fact from *outside the book under study*; anything needing another canonical book, an external homiletic image, or contemporary application is exposition and belongs in `### Alongside this` (§9).

Write warm, flowing commentary under the **Observations** label: after the Translation Barrier when one was earned, directly after **What it says** when none was. The prose should be clear, thoughtful, and accessible. Weave brief Tier 1 glosses naturally into the sentences when needed. Focus on what the text means and why it matters.

### 8\. Unbroken Witness

A Witness belongs to the verse block it illuminates. Place it after that block's **Observations**, before the next verse block. It separates the exegeted verses from the Witness's verses: the chapter's text stays in its blockquotes, the Witness's text stays in the Witness block — the two sets of verses are never mixed. A Witness that serves the whole major section rather than one block follows the final block's unit.

**Print:** a Witness unit prints the supplied English version only. The original-language text is checked, not printed — not in Greek or Hebrew letters, and not in transliteration. This holds for Witnesses placed at Integration and for intra-book Witnesses placed in Stage 3.

Use a clean `### Unbroken Witness` header, with no bold inside the heading. A sample page that writes `### **Unbroken Witness**` is not the pattern. Do not add an italic line under the heading explaining why the block is there. The heading is the label. It is not a section of its own, and not a break in the dialogue. A first-time reader has not seen this heading before; the `###` header is a smaller signal than the section's `##`, so placed after its block's **Observations** it reads as that block's close. Do not place it in the middle of the **Observations**. Unbroken \= each witness is quoted as one continuous block — bold reference, then the quotation, then a single closing comment; commentary never breaks the quotation.

**Preferred structure:**

### Unbroken Witness

**1 Corinthians 7:39** “A wife is bound as long as her husband lives; but if her husband dies, she is free to be married to whom she wishes, only in the Lord.” The same legal principle appears in almost identical language.

**Romans 6:9 and 6:14** “Death no longer is master over Him… sin shall not be master over you.” The verb of mastery returns in both places, binding the illustration to the larger argument of the letter.

Each entry begins with the bolded reference, moves immediately into the quoted portion or short paraphrase, and closes with a brief connecting comment. Leave a blank line between entries. Keep the block tight and scannable.

### 9\. Alongside this (exposition unit)

Not a mandatory slot, but an invited collaborative space at Integration. A section does not get one merely to fill a template; a chapter may have none, or several. The unit is produced during Integration in collaborative dialogue with the user — a hearing the user speaks there, or a note parked during Stage 3 and brought into the Integration conversation — and it is drafted warmly with the user. Stage 2 and Stage 3 do not write it. Standing Rules 3.3 is the gate; this section is the place.

Verses quoted in an Alongside this unit print the supplied English version only, as in a Witness (§8); the original-language text is checked, not printed.

If one is produced, place it with the verse block whose hearing it carries: after that block's **Observations**, and after its Witness if it has one. Use a clean `### Alongside this` header, with no extra bolding. A sample page that writes `### **Alongside this**` is not the pattern. The unit does not reprint the gate. The heading tells the reader the teacher is speaking. A paragraph that explains Lloyd-Jones, or the "and" of Scripture, is the rule talking, not the hearing. If a suggested hearing does not add to what is written, push back and name the miss. The user decides. Neither test is printed. Mark the unit once — never tag individual sentences. Keep the same warm teaching voice as the teaching; the heading does the distinguishing work.

**Preferred structure:**

### Alongside this

The families of Emanuel AME forgave the man who murdered their own — in public, while the grief was still raw. That is vacated space with skin on it. The coals of fire only fall from hands that have let go of the stones.

**Second specimen — a canonical room, not a contemporary image:**

### Alongside this

Leviticus 1 knew only dead sacrifices — the whole animal went up in smoke. Paul moves a living man into that room: the sacrifice that stays breathing. The old room was standing open; the verse walks into it.

The slot holds either kind: the teacher's image or the canon's open room. Canonical echoes that survived Rule 3.2 live in the Witness blocks above, not here — this unit is the teacher's hearing: images, counsel, and applications that passed the "and" of Scripture (not against, not past). What it never holds is a new claim. A 3.3 close is not that claim.

## III. Key Principles

### Clustering

The major section names its whole range once. Quotations are distributed at the joints (matching the Stage 2 groups), each with its own Barrier, so the section does not become one Barrier farm. A thematic title is a help on a long cluster, not a demand (section 4). A cluster can be small when the content requires deeper treatment.

### Translation Barriers

Use them to clear away common misunderstandings. Bold the relevant English words in the quoted text, **What it says**, and the barrier. Prioritize clarity and helpfulness over technical precision.

### Inline Glosses (Tier 1\)

Short, natural parenthetical explanations woven into the sentence. Use sparingly.

### Greek and Hebrew in the Teaching

Use sparingly in the running prose. Most detailed lexical work belongs in the Translation Barriers. Always transliterated, with long vowels marked (§6).

### Terminology

Prefer “family of God,” “the family,” “God’s people,” “the brethren,” or “our shared life” when the emphasis is relational belonging. “Household” remains valid when the text itself uses it or when structural realities are in view.

## IV. Visual and Formatting Guidelines

- Use clean, readable Markdown with semantic headings, spacing, and bullets.  
- Never use redundant Markdown (bold tags inside heading tags).  
- Strictly avoid LaTeX; rely on Unicode.  
- Avoid dense walls of text.  
- Keep Translation Barriers in a simple vertical bulleted list with minimal extra whitespace.  
- Aim for formatting that remains pristine when copied into Google Docs or Word.
