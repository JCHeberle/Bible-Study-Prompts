# Exegesis Style and Format Guide (07-28-2026)

## Purpose

This guide is designed to help produce clear, warm, and faithful biblical exposition for modern English-speaking believers. The goal is to do careful work with the original text while presenting it in an accessible, thoughtful, and spiritually nourishing way — without unnecessary technical language or an overly heavy 1st-century framing in the final prose.

### A Note on Intent and Use of Structure

The elements described in this guide (Translation Barriers, Unbroken Witness, thematic titles, and other structural features) are **tools**, not requirements or goals in themselves. They exist to serve the reader’s understanding. Use them when they genuinely help clarify the meaning, significance, or impact of the text. Do not include them simply because the guide mentions them. The guiding question should always be: *Does this help the reader better understand what the passage is saying and why it matters?* Structure should follow purpose, not the other way around.

---

## I. Overall Tone and Voice

Write for **thoughtful modern believers**. The exposition should feel like warm, clear Bible study teaching — respectful of the text and serious about meaning, but conversational and accessible.

Avoid turning the running commentary into a technical grammatical analysis. Greek and Hebrew should appear mainly in the Translation Barriers or in brief, well-explained glosses when they significantly affect meaning. Use first-person plural language naturally (“we,” “us,” “our”) when it fits the flow of teaching.

**Teaching Bridge principle**

Whenever the English translation is familiar but the Greek carries a richer or more precise force, begin with the wording the reader already knows and trusts, then gently open it up. The reader should experience an “Ah-ha” of deeper understanding rather than a sudden drop into unfamiliar language.

Gentle, caring confrontation is appropriate when the text itself is searching or when a common gap between the text’s claim and ordinary Christian experience needs to be named. Avoid both accusation and vague moralizing.

---

## II. Document Structure and Hierarchy

**The "No Redundant Formatting" Rule:** To ensure clean portability across Google Docs, Word, and standard text editors, never combine Markdown heading tags with bolding tags. A heading (`#`, `##`, `###`) already dictates weight and hierarchy; adding `**` inside a heading creates visual clutter and rendering errors.

### 1\. Main Title

Use a clear, descriptive title with a single hash (`#`) and no bold tags. When working with shorter passages or specific sections, include the verse range.

* **Correct:** `# Romans 6:1–2: Dead to Sin`  
    
* **Incorrect:** `# **Romans 6:1-2: Dead to Sin**`

### 2\. Introduction (Apostolic Lens)

Open with an introduction under `## Apostolic Lens` that sets the context and orients the reader. The length and content should be determined by the needs of the passage. The introduction is flexible and may incorporate other structural elements when they serve understanding.

### 3\. Major Sections

Divide the passage using Roman numeral headings (`##`). These should reflect the major movements or turning points in the text. Prefer full descriptive titles that help the reader see the movement of the argument.

* **Correct:** `## I. The Law's Jurisdiction Ends with Death`  
    
* **Incorrect:** `## **I. The Law's Jurisdiction Ends with Death**`

### 4\. Thematic Titles Inside a Section

Short descriptive titles that mark movements within the exposition should be set in simple **bold** text. Do not use Markdown heading tags (`###`) for these inline markers. When a verse range belongs with the thematic title, keep both in bold on the same line.

**Preferred pattern:**

`**Through the Body of the Messiah** **(Romans 7:4)**`

### 

### 5\. Quoted Text

Use the **NASB 2020** (unless another translation is specified). Place the full text of the cluster inside a single standard Markdown blockquote (`>`).

* **Bold** any English words in the quoted text that also appear in the Translation Barrier below it. This visual link is required.  
    
* Use clean Markdown formatting so that the blockquotes copy cleanly into standard word processors.

### 6\. Translation Barrier

Place this section directly after the quoted text. Use a clean vertical bulleted list with minimal extra whitespace between items. Focus on Tier 2 and Tier 3 words — terms that carry significant theological weight or where the English translation may flatten or distort the Greek meaning.

* Strictly avoid LaTeX formatting. Use standard **Unicode** for all Greek, Hebrew, and transliterated special characters.  
    
* Follow the Unicode with a clear, thought-for-thought explanation. Keep the explanations concise and readable.  
    
* **Bold** the same English words in the barrier that were bolded in the quoted text above.

**Preferred form of each bullet:**

`* **Law** (*nomos*) — here the concrete body of commandments that exercises real authority over those who live under it; not an abstract “principle” or “system,” but the living demand that rules the person.`

### 7\. Exposition

Write warm, flowing commentary beneath the Translation Barrier. The prose should be clear, thoughtful, and accessible. Weave brief Tier 1 glosses naturally into the sentences when needed. Focus on what the text means and why it matters.

### 8\. Unbroken Witness

At the end of a cluster or major section, include an **Unbroken Witness** block using a clean `### Unbroken Witness` header (no extra bolding).

**Preferred structure:**

### Unbroken Witness

**1 Corinthians 7:39** “A wife is bound as long as her husband lives; but if her husband dies, she is free to be married to whom she wishes, only in the Lord.” The same legal principle appears in almost identical language.

**Romans 6:9 and 6:14** “Death no longer is master over Him… sin shall not be master over you.” The verb of mastery returns in both places, binding the illustration to the larger argument of the letter.

Each entry begins with the bolded reference, moves immediately into the quoted portion or short paraphrase, and closes with a brief connecting comment. Leave a blank line between entries. Keep the block tight and scannable.

## III. Key Principles

### Clustering

Group verses thematically when possible. A cluster can be small when the content requires deeper treatment.

### Translation Barriers

Use them to clear away common misunderstandings. Bold the relevant English words in both the quoted text and the barrier. Prioritize clarity and helpfulness over technical precision.

### Inline Glosses (Tier 1\)

Short, natural parenthetical explanations woven into the sentence. Use sparingly.

### Greek and Hebrew in the Exposition

Use sparingly in the running prose. Most detailed lexical work belongs in the Translation Barriers.

### Terminology

Prefer “family of God,” “the family,” “God’s people,” or “our shared life” when the emphasis is relational belonging. “Household” remains valid when the text itself uses it or when structural realities are in view.

---

## IV. Visual and Formatting Guidelines

* Use clean, readable Markdown with semantic headings, spacing, and bullets.  
    
* Never use redundant Markdown (bold tags inside heading tags).  
    
* Strictly avoid LaTeX; rely on Unicode.  
    
* Avoid dense walls of text.  
    
* Keep Translation Barriers in a simple vertical bulleted list with minimal extra whitespace.  
    
* Aim for formatting that remains pristine when copied into Google Docs or Word.

## V. The Binding Visual Precedent (Required Model)

The following is the **exact** visual and structural pattern that must be followed for every Stage 3 Progressive Exposition. This single section is sufficient for a cold start. Mimic its layout, spacing, hierarchy, and rhythm precisely.

## I. The One Who Judges Stands Without Defense

**(Romans 2:1–5)**

**Therefore you are without defense, O man, every one of you who is judging.**

> Therefore you are **without defense**, O man, every one of you who is **judging**. For in the very act of **judging** the other, you are **condemning** yourself, because you who **judge** are practicing the same things. And we know that the **judgment** of God is **according to truth** upon those who practice such things. Do you **calculate** this, O man who **judges** those who practice such things and yet does them yourself, that you will escape the **judgment** of God? Or do you despise the riches of His **kindness** and **forbearance** and **long-suffering**, not recognizing that the **kindness** of God is leading you to **repentance**? But in accordance with your hardness and unrepentant heart you are storing up for yourself **wrath** in a day of **wrath** and of revelation of the **righteous judgment** of God,

**Translation Barrier**

* **without defense** (*anapologētos*) — the courtroom has already closed any possibility of an answering speech; the person stands stripped of any plea.  
    
* **judgment / to judge / to condemn** (*krima / krinō / katakrinō*) — the same root family that binds the act of judging others to the verdict that falls on the judge.  
    
* **according to truth** (*kata alētheian*) — according to reality as it actually is, not a vague sense of fairness.  
    
* **calculate** (*logizomai*) — to reckon, to work out in the mind as one works out an account.  
    
* **kindness / forbearance / long-suffering** (*chrēstotēs / anochē / makrothymia*) — layered riches: generous character, the deliberate holding-back of judgment already due, and the long-tempered endurance that refuses to strike at once.  
    
* **repentance** (*metanoia*) — a change of mind that turns the whole direction of life.  
    
* **wrath** (*orgē*) — the settled, holy opposition of God against what is wrong.  
    
* **righteous judgment** (*dikaiokrisia*) — the just crisis or decision that belongs uniquely to God.

Paul does not begin with a polite invitation. He begins with a verdict already rendered: you stand without defense. The moment you take the seat of the judge and measure someone else, the same measuring stick turns back on you. There is no delay, no separate hearing. The act of judging and the act of practicing the same things happen at the same time, in the same person. The one who points the finger is already caught in the very behavior he condemns.

We know this is how God’s judgment works. It is not a vague sense of fairness. It sits according to truth—according to what is actually real. It rests upon those who practice such things, not upon those who merely hear about them or disapprove of them in others.

So Paul presses the question home with the force of a ledger being opened: Do you really calculate that you will be the exception? Do you work it out in your mind that the judgment you acknowledge falls on others will somehow pass over you? The second-person singular is pointed and personal. There is no crowd to hide in.

The same voice now presses further. The question is not theoretical. It is personal and sharp: Do you despise the riches of His kindness, His forbearance, and His long-suffering? These are not three interchangeable virtues. They form a layered picture. Kindness is the generous character itself. Forbearance is the active restraint that keeps judgment from falling the moment it is deserved. Long-suffering is the extended patience that continues to wait. Together they are “riches”—overflowing, costly, and already displayed.

Yet the one being addressed is scorning them. While this scorning continues, there is an ongoing non-recognition: a willful, continuing refusal to see what is already in plain view. The kindness of God is leading toward *metanoia*—not the common English sense of feeling bad about past actions or being emotionally distressed over sin, but a genuine change of mind that reorients the whole direction of a life. It is not passive. It is already at work, drawing the heart toward that decisive turn.

Instead of yielding to that leading, the heart remains hard and unrepentant. In keeping with that condition, the person is actively storing up wrath for himself. The image is commercial and ironic: a personal treasure-hoard is being amassed, only the treasure is wrath. It is being laid up against the day of wrath and of the revelation of God’s righteous judgment.

### Unbroken Witness

**Matthew 7:1–2** “Do not judge so that you will not be judged. For in the way you judge, you will be judged; and by your standard of measure, it will be measured to you.” The same standard Jesus laid down returns upon the one who measures.

**Psalm 50:16–21** God confronts the one who recites His statutes with the mouth while practicing the opposite with the hands. The exposure is the same.

**Romans 1:32** Those who practice such things are worthy of death, yet they both do them and approve of those who do them. The mouth that judges is already being stopped.

**Exodus 34:6–7** “Yahweh, Yahweh, a God compassionate and gracious, slow to anger, and abundant in lovingkindness and truth… yet He will by no means leave the guilty unpunished.” The patience is real; the eventual repayment is equally real.

This single section is the binding visual model. Every future Stage 3 exposition must follow this exact pattern of hierarchy, spacing, thematic lead-in, continuous blockquote, Translation Barrier form, and Unbroken Witness rhythm.
