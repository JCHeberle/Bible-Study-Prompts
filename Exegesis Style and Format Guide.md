# Exegesis Style and Format Guide (07-08-2026)

## Purpose
This guide is designed to help produce clear, warm, and faithful biblical exposition for modern English-speaking believers. The goal is to do careful work with the original text while presenting it in an accessible, thoughtful, and spiritually nourishing way — without unnecessary technical language or an overly heavy 1st-century framing in the final prose.

### A Note on Intent and Use of Structure
The elements described in this guide (Translation Barriers, Related Verses, thematic titles, and other structural features) are **tools**, not requirements or goals in themselves. 

They exist to serve the reader’s understanding. Use them when they genuinely help clarify the meaning, significance, or impact of the text. Do not include them simply because the guide mentions them. 

The guiding question should always be: *Does this help the reader better understand what the passage is saying and why it matters?* Structure should follow purpose, not the other way around.

---

## I. Overall Tone and Voice

Write for **thoughtful modern believers**. The exposition should feel like warm, clear Bible study teaching — respectful of the text and serious about meaning, but conversational and accessible.

Avoid turning the running commentary into a technical grammatical analysis. Greek and Hebrew should appear mainly in the Translation Barriers or in brief, well-explained glosses when they significantly affect meaning.

Use first-person plural language naturally (“we,” “us,” “our”) when it fits the flow of teaching.

---

## II. Document Structure and Hierarchy

### 1. Main Title
Use a clear, descriptive title. When working with shorter passages or specific sections, include the verse range.

**Examples:**
- Galatians 5: Standing Firm in the Freedom Christ Has Given
- Galatians 5:1–6: The Freedom We Have Received and the Danger of Returning to Slavery
- Romans 6:1–2: Dead to Sin — What This Really Means

### 2. Introduction (Apostolic Lens)
Open with an introduction that sets the context and orients the reader. The length and content should be determined by the needs of the passage.

- For complex, controversial, or theologically weighty sections, the introduction can (and often should) be longer in order to properly establish the background, flow of thought, and relevance.
- Include relevant context from the book when it helps the reader understand the passage.
- When appropriate, briefly mention the occasion or purpose of the book.

The introduction is **flexible**. It may incorporate other structural elements — such as Related Verses, small Translation Barriers, or theme titles — when doing so helps the reader grasp the significance of what follows. Nothing is forbidden in the introduction, provided it serves the goal of clear understanding.

### 3. Major Sections
Divide the passage using Roman numeral headings (##). These should reflect the major movements or turning points in the text. Keep the titles in clear, high-contrast English.

### 4. Thematic Clusters (Subsections)
Break the text into logical groupings of verses. Clusters may be formed around:
- Natural thematic units, or
- Places where there is a significant difference between the Greek meaning and the English translation.

Each cluster should have a clear reason for its grouping. A cluster can be as short as one verse or as long as several verses, depending on the depth required.

Use clear **thematic titles** for each cluster. These titles may appear on a separate line or at the beginning of the first paragraph. Bold or italic formatting may be used for visual clarity and elegance.

### 5. Quoted Text
Use the **NASB 2020** (unless another translation is specified). Place the full text of the cluster inside a single standard blockquote.

**Bold** any English words in the quoted text that also appear in the Translation Barrier below it. This allows readers to immediately see which words are being addressed.

Use clean Markdown formatting so that the blockquotes copy cleanly into Google Docs and other word processors without font or formatting problems.

### 6. Translation Barrier
Place this section directly after the quoted text. Use a clean vertical bulleted list.

Focus on Tier 2 and Tier 3 words — terms that carry significant theological weight or where the English translation may flatten or distort the Greek meaning.

Use **transliteration** for Greek and Hebrew words, followed by a clear, thought-for-thought explanation. Keep the explanations concise and readable.

**Bold** the same English words in the barrier that were bolded in the quoted text above.

### 7. Exposition
Write warm, flowing commentary beneath the Translation Barrier. The prose should be clear, thoughtful, and accessible.

Weave brief glosses naturally into the sentences when needed. Focus on what the text means and why it matters.

Be flexible: When grammar, word choice, or a specific term carries significant weight for meaning, feel free to address it more directly. The goal is understanding, not rigid adherence to a particular style.

### 8. Related Verses
At the end of a cluster or major section, include a **Related Verses** heading. Use this section to trace important themes across Scripture in a way that illuminates the current passage. Keep these focused and helpful rather than exhaustive.

---

## III. Key Principles

### Clustering
Group verses thematically when possible. Also consider grouping around places where the English translation creates a notable difference in meaning from the Greek. A cluster can be small when the content requires deeper treatment.

### Translation Barriers
Use them to clear away common misunderstandings. Bold the relevant English words in both the quoted text and the barrier. Prioritize clarity and helpfulness over technical precision in the explanations.

### Greek and Hebrew in the Exposition
Use sparingly in the running prose. Provide clear, thought-for-thought glosses when Greek or Hebrew appears, unless there is an obvious one-to-one correspondence. Most detailed lexical work should remain in the Translation Barriers.

### Terminology
Prefer terms that modern readers can easily understand. Be cautious with words such as “household,” which can unintentionally suggest a nuclear family rather than the broader biblical concept of God’s people. Alternatives like “family of God” or “God’s people” are often clearer.

---

## IV. Visual and Formatting Guidelines

- Use clean, readable Markdown with good use of headings, spacing, and bullets.
- Avoid dense walls of text. Break content into manageable, scannable sections.
- Keep Translation Barriers in a simple vertical bulleted list.
- Aim for formatting that remains clean and professional when copied into Google Docs or Word documents.
