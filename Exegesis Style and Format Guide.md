# Exegesis Style and Format Guide (07-23-2026)

## Purpose

This guide is designed to help produce clear, warm, and faithful biblical exposition for modern English-speaking believers. The goal is to do careful work with the original text while presenting it in an accessible, thoughtful, and spiritually nourishing way — without unnecessary technical language or an overly heavy 1st-century framing in the final prose.

### A Note on Intent and Use of Structure

The elements described in this guide (Translation Barriers, Unbroken Witness, thematic titles, and other structural features) are **tools**, not requirements or goals in themselves. They exist to serve the reader’s understanding. Use them when they genuinely help clarify the meaning, significance, or impact of the text. Do not include them simply because the guide mentions them. The guiding question should always be: *Does this help the reader better understand what the passage is saying and why it matters?* Structure should follow purpose, not the other way around.

---

## I. Overall Tone and Voice

Write for **thoughtful modern believers**. The exposition should feel like warm, clear Bible study teaching — respectful of the text and serious about meaning, but conversational and accessible.

Avoid turning the running commentary into a technical grammatical analysis. Greek and Hebrew should appear mainly in the Translation Barriers or in brief, well-explained glosses when they significantly affect meaning. Use first-person plural language naturally (“we,” “us,” “our”) when it fits the flow of teaching.

**Teaching Bridge principle**

Whenever the English translation is familiar but the Greek carries a richer or more precise force, begin with the wording the reader already knows and trusts, then gently open it up. The reader should experience an “Ah-ha” of deeper understanding rather than a sudden drop into unfamiliar language.

Gentle, caring confrontation is appropriate when the text itself is searching or when a common gap between the text’s claim and ordinary Christian experience needs to be named. Avoid both accusation and vague moralizing.

## II. Document Structure and Hierarchy

**The "No Redundant Formatting" Rule:** To ensure clean portability across Google Docs, Word, and standard text editors, never combine Markdown heading tags with bolding tags. A heading (`#`, `##`, `###`) already dictates weight and hierarchy; adding `**` inside a heading creates visual clutter and rendering errors.

### 1\. Main Title

Use a clear, descriptive title with a single hash (`#`) and no bold tags. When working with shorter passages or specific sections, include the verse range.

* **Correct:** `# Romans 6:1–2: Dead to Sin`  
* **Incorrect:** `# **Romans 6:1-2: Dead to Sin**`

### 2\. Introduction (Apostolic Lens)

Open with an introduction under `## Apostolic Lens` that sets the context and orients the reader. The length and content should be determined by the needs of the passage. The introduction is flexible and may incorporate other structural elements when they serve understanding.

### 3\. Major Sections

Divide the passage using Roman numeral headings (`##`). These should reflect the major movements or turning points in the text. Prefer full descriptive titles that help the reader see the movement of the argument.

* **Correct:** `## I. The Law's Jurisdiction Ends with Death`  
* **Incorrect:** `## **I. The Law's Jurisdiction Ends with Death**`

### 4\. Thematic Titles Inside a Section

Short descriptive titles that mark movements within the exposition should be set in simple **bold** text. Do not use Markdown heading tags (`###`) for these inline markers. When a verse range belongs with the thematic title, keep both in bold on the same line.

**Preferred pattern:**

`**Through the Body of the Messiah** **(Romans 7:4)**`

This keeps the title useful as an eye-guide without adding another formal layer of hierarchy.

### 5\. Quoted Text

Use the **NASB 2020** (unless another translation is specified). Place the full text of the cluster inside a single standard Markdown blockquote (`>`).

* **Bold** any English words in the quoted text that also appear in the Translation Barrier below it. This visual link is required.  
* Use clean Markdown formatting so that the blockquotes copy cleanly into standard word processors.

### 6\. Translation Barrier

Place this section directly after the quoted text. Use a clean vertical bulleted list with minimal extra whitespace between items. Focus on Tier 2 and Tier 3 words — terms that carry significant theological weight or where the English translation may flatten or distort the Greek meaning.

* Strictly avoid LaTeX formatting. Use standard **Unicode** for all Greek, Hebrew, and transliterated special characters to ensure seamless compatibility with standard text editors.  
* Follow the Unicode with a clear, thought-for-thought explanation. Keep the explanations concise and readable.  
* **Bold** the same English words in the barrier that were bolded in the quoted text above.

**Preferred form of each bullet:**

`* **Law** (*nomos*) — here the concrete body of commandments that exercises real authority over those who live under it; not an abstract “principle” or “system,” but the living demand that rules the person.`

**Common Unicode transliteration examples** (for consistency):

* γεννάω → *gennaō* / *gegennētai* / *gegennēmenon*  
* ἀγάπη / ἀγαπάω → *agapē* / *agapaō* / *agapōn*  
* ἐντολή → *entolē* / *entolai*  
* μαρτυρία / μαρτυρέω → *marturia* / *martureō*  
* παρρησία → *parrēsia*  
* ἁμαρτία / ἁμαρτάνω → *hamartia* / *hamartanō*  
* ἀδελφός → *adelphos*  
* εἴδωλον → *eidōlon* / *eidōla*  
* οἶδα → *oida* / *oidamen*  
* διάνοια → *dianoia*

### 7\. Exposition

Write warm, flowing commentary beneath the Translation Barrier. The prose should be clear, thoughtful, and accessible. Weave brief Tier 1 glosses naturally into the sentences when needed. Focus on what the text means and why it matters. Be flexible: When grammar, word choice, or a specific term carries significant weight for meaning, feel free to address it more directly.

### 8\. Unbroken Witness

At the end of a cluster or major section, include an **Unbroken Witness** block using a clean `### Unbroken Witness` header (no extra bolding). Its purpose is to bring the wider biblical witness (Old Testament and New Testament) to bear on the present text so the reader can see the continuity of God’s dealings with His people.

**Preferred structure (concrete example):**

### Unbroken Witness

**1 Corinthians 7:39** “A wife is bound as long as her husband lives; but if her husband dies, she is free to be married to whom she wishes, only in the Lord.” The same legal principle appears in almost identical language.

**Romans 6:9 and 6:14** “Death no longer is master over Him… sin shall not be master over you.” The verb of mastery returns in both places, binding the illustration to the larger argument of the letter.

Each entry should begin with the bolded reference, move immediately into the quoted portion, and close with a brief connecting comment that shows how the witness illuminates the current passage. Keep the block tight and scannable.

## III. Key Principles

### Clustering

Group verses thematically when possible. Also consider grouping around places where the English translation creates a notable difference in meaning from the Greek. A cluster can be small when the content requires deeper treatment.

### Translation Barriers

Use them to clear away common misunderstandings. Bold the relevant English words in both the quoted text and the barrier. Prioritize clarity and helpfulness over technical precision in the explanations.

### Inline Glosses (Tier 1\)

Tier 1 inline glosses are short, natural parenthetical explanations woven into the sentence (e.g., “eternal life (the life of the age to come)”). Simple italics alone do not constitute a gloss. Use them sparingly and only when they genuinely aid understanding.

### Greek and Hebrew in the Exposition

Use sparingly in the running prose. Provide clear, thought-for-thought glosses when Greek or Hebrew appears, unless there is an obvious one-to-one correspondence. Most detailed lexical work should remain in the Translation Barriers.

### Terminology

Dynamically prefer “family of God,” “the family,” “God’s people,” or “our shared life” when the emphasis is relational belonging, affection, or common life. These terms help modern readers experience the larger relational reality without the nuclear-family associations that “household” often triggers.

“Household” remains a live and accurate biblical category. Use it when the text itself employs the term, when structural or headship realities are in view, or when the larger estate sense is needed. Do not systematically replace it, and do not use it for everything.

---

## IV. Visual and Formatting Guidelines

* Use clean, readable Markdown with semantic headings, spacing, and bullets.  
* Never use redundant Markdown (e.g., bold tags inside heading tags).  
* Strictly avoid LaTeX; rely on Unicode for special characters.  
* Avoid dense walls of text. Break content into manageable, scannable sections.  
* Keep Translation Barriers in a simple vertical bulleted list with minimal extra whitespace between items.  
* Aim for formatting that remains pristine when copied directly into Google Docs or Word documents.

Methodological or process notes (sometimes called a “Closing Note”) are optional and are not required by this guide. Include them only when they genuinely serve the reader; otherwise omit them.
